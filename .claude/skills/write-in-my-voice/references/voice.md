# Christine Tham's writing voice

Drawn from four pieces spanning thirty-three years and three registers: the 1987 honours thesis
(`rubato`), a technical history (`unix-50-celebration`, 2020), and two personal essays
(`how-to-avoid-folo...`, 2017 and `why-i-consider-myself-to-be-retired...`, 2019).

The register shifts a great deal between these. The constants are what matter, and they are
listed first.

## The constants

**Personal experience carries the argument, it does not decorate it.** Even the history of Unix
is told through her own career: which machine she sat in front of, what she was paid, who rang
her up. A claim usually arrives attached to the moment she learned it. This is the single most
important thing to get right, because it is what most obviously separates her prose from a
competent essay about the same subject.

**Concrete specifics, constantly.** Not "an early minicomputer" but "a VAX 11/780". Not "quite
cheap" but "\$10 an hour". Named machines, named companies, named people, real prices, real
dates, real sizes. When she generalises, she has almost always just earned it with a particular.

**Short paragraphs.** Median 36 words in the essays, and even the technical piece rarely runs
past a hundred. A paragraph is usually one idea, and when the idea is important it gets a
paragraph of its own, sometimes one sentence long.

**Candour about herself, including the unflattering.** "In hindsight, I was incredibly naive.
Sure, I was smart. But I was pretty arrogant too." Weight, blood pressure, depression,
foolishness, being forgiven for hacking a university. She does not protect her own image, and
that candour is what buys the reader's trust for the advice.

**Dry understatement rather than emphasis.** "Needless to say I accepted the offer immediately."
"I meekly suggested I was free tomorrow afternoon." "I think they forgave me." The funny line is
delivered flat and then left alone. She does not point at her own jokes.

**Endings land short and often turn aphoristic.** "It's amazing how one word can completely
change a life." "When I read it, it changed my life." The closing move is usually a compression
of the whole piece into one plain sentence, arrived at rather than announced.

Treat these as the shape to land inside, not a target to hit exactly. The two that behave like

not in her voice no matter what else it does.

## Register

Decide which of three modes the piece is in before drafting, because paragraph length,
parenthetical density and formality all follow from it.

**Personal essay.** Advice, career, life. Second person address, rhetorical questions, remembered
dialogue, short paragraphs, an argument built from her own history. `how-to-avoid-folo` and
`why-i-consider-myself-to-be-retired` are the models.

**Technical or historical.** Explaining a system, a period, a project. Longer paragraphs, heavy
parenthetical asides carrying the technical detail, still narrated through her own involvement.
`unix-50-celebration` is the model.

**Formal or academic.** A paper or thesis. Careful hedged claims, passive constructions where the
work rather than the worker is the subject, citations. `rubato` is the model. Rarely wanted for a
blog article, but useful when reproducing or extending a paper.

## Structure and argument

**Headings are plain descriptions or real questions.** "What is Unix?", "Early years
(1970-1980)", "Unix in Academia (early 1980s)", "Happiness as a State of Mind", "Unrealistic
Career Expectations", "C'mon really? Does that strategy even work?" Note the last one: when she
anticipates the reader's scepticism she puts it in the heading, in the reader's own words, then
answers it immediately underneath. ("Yes, really.")

**Rhetorical question, then answer, is the main engine of transition.** "Okay, so you have FOLO
and you are desperately unhappy. What can you do about it?" The question is usually asked on the
reader's behalf, not the writer's.

**She anticipates and voices objections in quotation marks**, then concedes before answering:

> Okay, you say. "I hear you, but I still want to be more successful …"
>
> Fair enough.
>
> But let's examine what your expectations are.

**Remembered dialogue is set as blockquotes**, not woven into the paragraph. The retirement essay
is built almost entirely from a sequence of quoted phone calls, each one advancing the plot, with
her reaction between them. If a piece has conversation in it, use this.

**Numbered or day-by-day lists to structure an argument.** The FOLO essay gives a naive Day 1 to
Day 5 plan, then a better Day 1 to Day 8 plan. She likes the parallel structure of a wrong version
and a right version presented in the same shape.

**Bulleted lists lead with an italicised claim, then explain it.** From the thesis: "_Rubato
programs are compact._ Writing a _Rubato_ program is just like…"

**Capitalised abstract nouns carry weight.** "Happiness is a State of Mind." "It is a State of
Mind." "I still have Ambition." "we were a Happy Family." Sparing, deliberate, and a genuine tic
worth reproducing when a concept is being elevated.

## Sentence-level habits

**Open sentences with conjunctions freely.** And, But, So, Or, Also, However, Indeed, Besides,
Yet, Perhaps, Eventually, Finally. About one sentence in ten.

**Parenthetical asides are a signature**, especially in technical writing, and they can be long:

> a VAX 11/780 (a mini-computer that was fast in its day, and capable of executing 1 million
> instructions per second, but very slow by today's standards, hundreds of times slower than even
> the least capable Raspberry Pi)

They carry three jobs: the technical gloss, the wry qualification ("which subsequently failed",
"because he claimed he can afford the parking tickets"), and the parenthetical correction of her
own younger self.

**Single-sentence paragraphs mark the turns.** "Suddenly I was without work and without health."
"Fair enough." "The moment he said that, I was in." "I was ecstatic." Used at the pivot of a
story, never for ordinary emphasis.

**Plain verbs and an unshowy vocabulary.** She reaches for a fancy word rarely, and when she does
it is usually precise rather than decorative ("Byzantine", "aegis", "idiosyncronistic").

**First person plural for the household, first person singular for the work.** "We had calculated
that we have accumulated enough savings" (her and her partner) against "I announced my
professional retirement".

**Tense slips into the present inside anecdotes** and she leaves it there. "He drove a Porsche,
which he parks illegally every day". Do not tidy this up if it arises naturally; it is part of
how the anecdotes sound spoken.

## Punctuation and spelling

- **No em dashes.** Zero across the whole corpus. Where a dash is wanted she uses a spaced en
  dash ( – ) or a spaced hyphen ( - ). This is the single most reliable tell of AI-written prose
  in her name.
- **Australian spelling throughout**, in every modern piece. `-ise` not `-ize` (realise,
  organise, recognise, minimise, emphasise), `-yse` not `-yze` (analyse, paralyse), `-our` not
  `-or` (colour, favour, behaviour, honour, labour), `-re` not `-er` (centre, metre, theatre),
  doubled `l` before a suffix (travelled, cancelled, marvellous) but single in skilful and
  fulfil, and the noun/verb split in licence/license and practice/practise. Also grey, ageing,
  sceptic, storey, enquiry, aluminium.

  Do not apply this by search and replace. Many `-ise` words are `-ise` in American English too
  (advertise, exercise, surprise, comprise, supervise), plenty of `-ize` strings are not the
  suffix at all (size, capsize, prize, seize), and `-our` drops before some suffixes: honour but
  honorary, humour but humorous, labour but laborious. Proper nouns and quoted material keep
  whatever spelling they came with.

  The 1987 thesis uses American `-ize` forms throughout, so match the era rather than the house
  style when reproducing a historical document.

- **"ie" and "eg" without full stops.**
- **Exclamation marks almost never**, and only for genuine delight: "(including system memory and
  I/O ports!)".
- **Currency and numbers written plainly.** In this repository a literal `$` must be escaped as
  `\$` or the Markdown processor reads the paragraph as maths.
- **Bold is rare.** She does not scatter bolded phrases through prose for skimmability. Italics do
  the emphatic work, usually on a term being introduced or a claim being made.

## What is absent

These are the things that will make a draft read as machine-written even when the facts are
right. Their absence from 253 sentences is evidence, not preference.

- **Em dashes.** None.
- **"It's not just X, it's Y"** and its relatives. None.
- **Tricolons for rhythm.** She uses three-part lists to enumerate, not to swell.
- **Signposting of structure.** No "In this article we will explore", no "Let's dive in", no
  recap section at the end that lists what was just said.
- **Bolded key phrases** sprinkled mid-paragraph.
- **Hedging stacks.** "It could be argued that it may perhaps be the case." She states things.
- **Enthusiasm as a substitute for detail.** No "incredibly powerful", "game-changing",
  "revolutionised". When something impresses her she says what it did: a Hello World that compiled
  to 4 kilobytes including the runtime.
- **Symmetrical paragraph lengths.** Her paragraphs are lumpy. Six words then sixty.
- **Summary paragraphs that restate the piece.** The ending is a turn, not a recap.
