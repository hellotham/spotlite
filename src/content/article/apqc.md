_Filed under the date APQC announced Version 7.0.0. The consulting work behind it ran for
several years either side._

Somewhere in most large organisations there is an argument about what things are called. One
division calls it procurement, another calls it sourcing, a third has it buried inside
operations, and none of them can be compared with any of the others because nobody agrees on
where one process stops and the next begins.

APQC's Process Classification Framework exists to end that argument. It is a taxonomy of what
an organisation does, arranged as a numbered hierarchy, deliberately written to be independent
of industry, region and size. At the top it has a dozen or so categories, and beneath them
more than a thousand process elements. If two companies both adopt it, they can finally
compare like with like.

I had a small hand in it, through consulting work at Transfield Services, and it is one of the
more unusual things on my CV.

## What the framework is

APQC is a non-profit that has been doing benchmarking and best-practice research since the
1970s. The Process Classification Framework began in 1992, when more than eighty organisations
came together to agree a common structure, and it went on to become the most downloaded thing
APQC has ever published.

Its value is entirely in being shared. A taxonomy that only you use is just your filing
system. The point of the PCF is that thousands of organisations use the same one, so a
conversation about the cost of a process can start from a common definition rather than from
forty minutes of establishing what each side means.

## Why Transfield needed one

Transfield Services, which later became Broadspectrum, had grown by acquisition and grown
fast. Its employee base had increased by 329 per cent, and by the time I was working with them
the company had more than 26,000 people across 20 industries and 11 countries.

Each acquisition had arrived with its own way of describing its own work. That is the ordinary
consequence of buying autonomous businesses, and it is not a problem until you want to compare
two of them, or consolidate a function, or work out what you are actually paying for something
across the group.

So Transfield brought in APQC's advisory team to build a standard process framework on top of
the PCF. The published result was a 30 per cent reduction in redundancy, which is a polite way
of saying that nearly a third of what the organisation thought were distinct processes turned
out to be the same processes wearing different names. Alongside it ran a Centres of Excellence
programme to make the new shared view stick, which is the harder half of any exercise like
this. APQC wrote it up as
[a case study](https://www.apqc.org/resource-library/resource-listing/creating-process-framework-transfield-services)
in May 2015.

## Contributing back

The interesting part, at least to me, is that this was not a one-way transaction.

A framework like the PCF is only as good as the practice it is drawn from, so APQC revises it
using input from the organisations actually applying it. When Version 6.0.0 was released in
July 2012, marking the framework's twentieth anniversary, APQC credited the update to
"significant input from APQC members, including Intel, IBM, and Transfield Services". Seeing
the company I was consulting to listed beside those two was a pleasant surprise. My own
contribution to that release was minor.

Version 7.0.0, released at APQC's Process Conference in October 2015, is where I did real
work. Our adviser there was Jeff Varney, a director at APQC, and it was Jeff who took our
suggestions and worked them into the release.

That version made one very visible change, which Jeff
[wrote up at the time](https://www.apqc.org/blog/giving-services-their-proper-place-pcf). It
split the old category 4.0, Deliver Products and Services, into two standalone categories, one
for physical products and one for services, taking the framework from twelve process
categories to thirteen. That particular suggestion came from IBM rather than from us, but it
is the change that renumbered everything after it, and it is why the business capabilities
category, previously 12.0, became 13.0.

Our contribution sits inside that last category. **Section 13.2, Manage Portfolio, Program,
and Project**, is the one I worked on.

## Why that section

Category 13.0 describes how an organisation gets better at what it does, rather than what it
does. Process management, portfolio and project management, quality, change management,
benchmarking and knowledge management all live there.

Section 13.2 fitted us because it was not theory. Jeff's own post makes the point that
services are typically delivered "through projects, continuous support functions, or discrete
instances based upon a request for service or incident", and that is precisely what Transfield
was. A company running maintenance and operations contracts across 20 industries and 11
countries is running a portfolio of programmes and projects, and that portfolio was the
mechanism by which the business made its money. We were describing something we did every day.

The section next door helped too. Section 13.5 covers developing and managing enterprise-wide
knowledge, which is what the Centres of Excellence programme was for. Writing a definition of
a process while running that process on 26,000 people is an unusually good way to find out
whether the definition is any use.

There is a nice symmetry in it, too. The 6.0.0 announcement had already singled out the
business capabilities section for "an extensive reworking and expansion", covering process
improvement, quality management, knowledge management and benchmarking. So the category I
ended up working inside in 7.0.0 was the one the previous release had already decided needed
the most attention.

## Why any of this matters

Taxonomies are unglamorous. Nobody has ever been thanked at a company meeting for agreeing a
numbering scheme, and the work of writing one is mostly argument about boundaries.

But a shared vocabulary is what makes measurement possible. Without it, benchmarking is people
comparing numbers that do not mean the same thing, process improvement is local and
unrepeatable, and every acquisition adds another dialect. The PCF is used in thousands of
organisations precisely because somebody did the boring work of agreeing where the lines go.

What I liked about it, and still like about it, is how ordinary the process was. A framework
downloaded by organisations all over the world does not descend from anywhere. It gets
revised, section by section, by practitioners who noticed that a definition did not survive
contact with 26,000 people, and said so. My name is nowhere on it, which is exactly as it
should be, but the section is a little better than it was.
